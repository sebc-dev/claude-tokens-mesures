On Monday morning, the team discovers that some of the invoices sent over the weekend show an amount of zero. Nobody has touched the billing module in weeks, and all the tests pass. The first reaction is to look for a culprit: a library update, a configuration change, a mistyped value. Yet the problem is simpler than it seems.

Reading through the logs, they notice that every affected invoice belongs to a recently created customer. These customers do not have a pricing category yet, and the function that computes the total returns zero when it cannot find a rate. The case had never been anticipated, because originally every customer received a category as soon as they signed up. A change to the sign-up form, a month ago, made that field optional.

The fix takes only a few lines, but the real lesson lies elsewhere. First, you need to write a test that reproduces the case before touching the code: a customer without a category, an invoice, and the expected amount. Next, you need to decide what the application should do in this situation. Should it apply a default rate, block the invoice from being sent, or alert someone? This is not a technical question, it is a business rule, and only the team can answer it.

Finally, you need to ask why the existing tests caught nothing. They did check the calculation, but always with complete customers, created from the same sample data. A test that only covers the expected cases does not protect against the unexpected ones. That is often where errors hide: not in the complicated code, but in the assumptions nobody wrote down.
